### 1. #10: A camera obscura tent holding an evensong litany of intercession for strangers crossing its live projected street, the officiant naming each passerby and the congregation answering 'Lord, have mercy' until daylight fails, because people seen once, inverted and unrecorded, can only be held by being prayed for while they pass.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1620 · feasibility 1431 · promise 1644
- **Cards:** real-time only, camera obscura, liturgy
- **Original:** A camera obscura tent-chapel that screens the live street as a film service, with a written order of call and response triggered by passersby, because a projection that cannot be recorded needs a ritual form to turn unrepeatable light into a shared event.

**prosecutor** (minor, subject): Without verified sources, no specific prior art can be named; the walk-in camera obscura and ritual-performance strands are likely each covered separately, but I found no evidence of their combination.

> I could not run a working web search, so I have no verified citations. The catalog has no work that links two of the cards. I cannot point to a source that combines them, so I am not claiming that this exact combination exists.

*Advocate rebuts:* Agreed, and it costs the idea nothing. Walk-in obscuras and ritualized live cinema each have precedent. No one has claimed this combination, and no verified source shows that it exists. The novelty is not that the strands are new. It is what the liturgy does to the projected strangers, and the revision makes that sharper.

**skeptic** (serious, subject): A portable tent that projects a readable daylight street needs a large, heavy lens and a long throw, so it is not the light-tight, portable chapel the statement describes.

> A useful outdoor camera obscura needs a large lens or aperture to throw a bright enough street image onto a table in daylight. A lens that size is heavy, costly and hard to keep light-tight inside a tent, so the portable, one-person chapel as described is a different and harder build than the idea assumes.

*Advocate rebuts:* This overstates the optics. Portable tent obscuras were sold commercially in the 18th century, and Kepler used one in the field. A roughly 100 mm meniscus lens with a 1.5–2 m focal length, under a 45° mirror in a rotating turret, throws a readable daylight street onto a white table. That is the Bristol and Dumfries design scaled down, and the lens is hand-held in weight and cost. A tall tent supplies the throw. Light-tightness comes from blackout fabric and a sleeved turret collar, which is a sewing problem, not an engineering one. It is buildable by one person on a small budget. The real optical limit is dusk, when the image fades. The revision uses that limit as the structure.

**taste_critic** (serious, card_reading `liturgy`): The liturgy is borrowed only as a scaffold of call and response, so the street's events become cute cues for murmured answers, and nothing is ever at stake, strange, or sacred.

> Meeting the finished thing: you duck into a dark tent, watch an upside-down street on a white table, read from a printed program, and murmur 'Amen' when a pigeon crosses. It's tasteful and polite, and it stays entirely within the art-school installation register of mindfulness. The cliché is 'attention as the real film': Cage's 4'33", slow-cinema contemplation, Quaker silence with a lens. Liturgy is read as pure format, with an order of service, responses and a dismissal, and none of its charge: no sin, grief, absolution, or dread. The street is treated as neutral and benign, so nothing risks anything. There is no uncanny misfire, such as the congregation answering a call that never comes, a response that sours when a funeral passes, or the street starting to answer back. There is no absurdity either, no commercial or pop debris to curdle the solemnity, and no heartbreak. The calls are generic ('a figure crossing, the light dimming'), so the ritual could be applied to any window. The rationale's claim that it 'trains attention' is a self-improvement pitch, and I distrust that. The result is a pleasant, earnest, forgettable afternoon.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded, and this is the critique that matters. I took liturgy's skeleton and left out its charge. 'Amen when a pigeon crosses' is mindfulness with a program sheet, and generic cues would fit any window. The better idea uses a liturgy that has stakes: a litany of intercession at evensong. Every stranger who crosses the inverted street is named aloud by what they carry and prayed for by people they will never know about and who will never see them again. Empty street means the call doesn't come, and the congregation must wait. A funeral, a fight or an ad truck enters the same litany unedited. The service ends when daylight can no longer form an image. Nothing here trains anyone. You plead for strangers you cannot keep, and that act is uncomfortably close to surveillance.

### 2. #6: A one-ink photocopied comic that tells a felled oak's life backward from saw cut to seedling, one panel per year sized to a cross-dated ring series, while its scans drive a granular patch built only from the felling's recording, because a tree's chronology becomes legible only once cut, so its death supplies the sound of its life.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1586 · feasibility 1489 · promise 1614
- **Cards:** one color, granular synthesis, dendrochronology
- **Original:** A one-ink risograph comic whose panel widths follow a real oak's cross-dated ring-width series and whose scanned pages drive a granular-synthesis patch, turning each year into a grain cloud, because rings and grains both store time as thin measured slices and single-color ink gives the scanner one clean density value, as a core sample does.

**prosecutor** (minor, rationale): Tree-ring-to-sound mappings and image-to-grain scores are each familiar, so the idea's novelty rests only on the comic-panel framing, and the claim that one ink gives the scanner a clean value is unproven.

> I could not run web searches because the search tool hit its use limit, so I have no verified URLs to cite. The catalog also holds no work linking any two of the cards. From general knowledge, the parts exist separately. Tree-ring sonification is an established practice, and so are image-as-score tools such as UPIC and Metasynth. Granular resynthesis driven by scanned images is also common. I cannot cite a specific work that combines them, so I am not claiming prior art for the full combination.

- No prior art found.

*Advocate rebuts:* Partly rebut. The separate parts (tree-ring sonification, image-as-score tools like UPIC and Metasynth) are familiar, and I never claimed otherwise. What is new is a page that works both as a readable narrative and as an exact score, with the grid itself being the chronology. The revision moves further from those precedents, since its sound source and story structure come from the felling, not from a generic mapping. I concede that 'one clean density value' is an engineering claim, not a proven one. But black toner on cream is about the easiest thing a scanner can threshold, and per-panel coverage is a few lines of Python averaging over a known panel box. That is easy to verify in an afternoon, not a weakness in the idea.

**skeptic** (serious, constraint): The risograph print run, which the builder's gear list omits, is the step that makes the single-ink page real and it needs an outside shop.

> The idea needs a cross-dated oak ring-width series, which the builder can only get from an archive or a collaborator, and a Pure Data grain engine driven by scanner output. The builder has Python and a Mac, so the patch is buildable, but a risograph press is not in the gear list. Printing a one-ink comic with panel widths tied to measured rings needs an outside print shop and several proof runs at real money.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded: I tied the one-color card to a risograph, which the card never asked for, and that put an outside shop and costly proofing on the critical path. The constraint is one color, not a particular press. A black-toner photocopied zine meets it literally, gives an even harder black for the scanner, and costs pocket change per proof. So I've dropped riso from the idea. The ring data still comes from a public archive such as the ITRDB, which is a download, not a collaboration.

**taste_critic** (serious, behavior): The idea is a tidy data-to-sound mapping that makes drought sparse and wet years lush, so the result is predictable, pleasant, nature-documentary ambience with no uncanny turn or emotional wound, and the felled-tree-as-archive heartbreak goes unused.

> Meeting the finished thing, you get a wordless nature comic about an oak, a scan-to-sound pipeline, and a gallery placard explaining that rings are like grains. The mapping is a data-sonification recipe: width becomes duration, darkness becomes density, drought becomes sparse and wet becomes lush. Every output is predictable from the input, so nothing in the work can surprise, curdle or ache. The soundtrack is wind in oak leaves, the most agreeable ambient sound there is, so the result is a tasteful drone that reads as a TED-talk-adjacent art-science collaboration. The comic is hostage to its data, since panel widths are fixed by a published series and the story is just a tree growing, surviving drought and recovering. That is an arc anyone could predict, and it has no uncanny turn, no absurdity and no loss. The one real heartbreak available, a tree's whole life as an archive that only becomes legible when it is cut down, is mentioned nowhere in the idea. As written it is a clever correspondence demo that wants admiration for its rigor and gives no dream or wound to remember. The earnest 'both store time as thin slices' rationale is the cliché: the pleasing analogy treated as if it were the work.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded, and this was the better idea hiding inside mine. Wind in oak leaves turned the piece into agreeable ambience, and a forward drought-and-recovery arc is predictable. The wound was sitting in the domain unused: rings only become legible once the tree is felled. The revision reverses the reading so the comic starts at the saw cut and walks inward to the seedling. It also makes the felling recording the only sound material, so every year of the tree's life is rebuilt from the grains of its death. The mapping stays rigorous, but now the rigor serves an ache: the further back you read, the further the saw dissolves, until the seedling is almost silence.

### 3. #3: A hand-drawn stop-motion short with no dialogue that maps, road by road, a village later drowned by a reservoir while a Standard MIDI File built from its old survey plays on a self-playing piano, because the file encodes each road's elevation as pitch, so the ear hears hills the flat drawing cannot show.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1512 · feasibility 1389 · promise 1562
- **Cards:** no dialogue, MIDI file, cartography
- **Original:** A wordless stop-motion short in which a pen plotter draws a map translated from a Standard MIDI File, filmed frame by frame and screened against that file's playback, because the file is already a time-stamped coordinate list, so one artifact yields both the image and a perfectly synced score.

**prosecutor** (serious, behavior): Plotting MIDI pitch and time as pen-plotter lines is already routine, so the idea's only novelty is relabelling the piano-roll plot as a map, and the claimed sync benefit is built into any MIDI visualiser.

> I found no catalog work linking these cards. On the web, two open-source projects already send MIDI to a pen plotter. jskjott/midi-plotter draws MIDI notes with pitch and duration as geometry. revdancatt/plotter-sounds uses MIDI and audio to drive plotter drawings. So the MIDI-to-plotted-line mapping, with pitch as one axis and time as the other, is an established hobbyist technique. Piano-roll notation is the same pitch-by-time plot, and the idea's core step is to read it as a map. What remains is the cartographic framing (tracks as roads, velocity as road class) and the stop-motion film wrapper. I did not find a film that combines them, but both halves have close neighbours. The synced-score rationale is also weak. Any plotter or piano-roll visualisation driven by one MIDI file already stays in sync with that file's playback.

- [GitHub - jskjott/midi-plotter: realtime midi visualisation with the pen plotter](https://github.com/jskjott/midi-plotter): Plots MIDI note pitch and duration as pen-plotter drawings, which is the idea's core MIDI-to-plotter mapping without the map framing or film.
- [GitHub - revdancatt/plotter-sounds: playing with midi and audio to control the pen plotter](https://github.com/revdancatt/plotter-sounds): Uses MIDI and audio input to drive pen-plotter generative drawings, so MIDI-controlled plotting is already established practice.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded. With pitch on one axis and ticks on the other, my 'map' was the piano roll that midi-plotter already draws, so the only new step was a relabelling. The fix reverses the direction: a real surveyed territory comes first, and the MIDI file is derived from it. The drawing then shows the roads in plan view, which is not a piano roll. The file carries elevation, so it is a separate measurement of the same place rather than a replot of the music.

**skeptic** (serious, behavior): The plotter's physical speed and pen lifts cannot keep pace with dense MIDI ticks, so frame-exact sync with playback is impossible without changing the music.

> A pen plotter moves at a fixed physical speed and must lift and lower its pen between strokes. Stop-motion means the plotter draws in shutter-driven steps, not in real time, so the drawn map cannot track the MIDI playback clock. The builder would need to write firmware that maps ticks to plotter moves, then hold the synth and the stepper timing together through a sync rig. Dense MIDI tracks will demand more pen travel per second of music than the plotter can physically manage, forcing the music to slow or the map to be simplified. That is a months-long firmware and mechanics problem on top of the plotter build.

*Advocate rebuts:* This misreads stop-motion. Shooting frame by frame removes any need for the plotter to keep pace with playback. Each tick converts to a frame number (tick to seconds, times 24), and I photograph whatever state the drawing should be in at that frame. Pen speed and pen lifts happen between exposures and never appear on screen. No sync rig or firmware is needed, only a frame table generated from the file. The revision also drops the plotter for a hand-drawn line, which removes the mechanics entirely.

**taste_critic** (serious, rationale): The 'map' is just a piano roll with renamed axes, so the film is a self-explaining process demo whose perfect machine sync leaves no tension, no place, and no uncanny gap between picture and sound.

> Meeting the finished thing, you watch a pen scratch a squiggly line across paper while a General MIDI piano plays the notes that are the line. It is a piano-roll visualizer filmed slowly. A piano roll is already pitch on one axis and time on the other, so calling the result a 'map' only renames the axes. The viewer never encounters a territory, a place, a landmark or a shape that means anything, because the image is the music's own graph and there is nothing uncanny in the gap between the two. The sync is the rationale's selling point, but it is guaranteed by construction. A guarantee gives no tension, no drift, no surprise. Nothing can go wrong, so nothing is felt. The cliché is the generative-art or data-sonification demo, where a process is the whole show and the output is whatever it happens to be. The General MIDI synth makes it worse. It is the stock, beige sound of every ringtone, and it is a missing opportunity for a dreamlike or strange sonic world. Stop-motion normally brings handmade warmth and a ghost of the animator's body. Here a machine plots the line, so the labor, the tactility and the human error that would make stop-motion tender are removed. The 'no dialogue' constraint costs nothing, since a plotter drawing to music never wanted words. The cartographic legend and reveal are asserted but never given anything to reveal. The concept is clever and the film is a lecture on its own method, a gimmick that explains itself in one sentence and is exhausted by the time you've understood it.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded, and this critique points to the better film. A graph of the music contains no place, and redundant picture and sound leave nothing to feel. The revision gives the map a real territory: a village drowned by a reservoir, so the reveal is of a place that no longer exists. The sound encodes elevation that the plan view cannot show, which opens an honest gap: the ear hears hills that the eye sees as flat lines. Hand animation restores the animator's body and errors. A self-playing piano replaces the beige General MIDI patch with keys moving under no hands, which suits a vanished village.
