### 1. #3: A candlelit church zoetrope whose soldier's walk cycle has one frame per name on the parish memorial, starting empty and cranked unbroken from the war diary's zero hour to its closing hour while each name answered 'Grant them eternal rest' slips its frame into the turning drum, because prayers for rest visibly raise a dead man into walking.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1642 · feasibility 1434 · promise 1644
- **Cards:** real-time only, zoetrope, liturgy
- **Lineage:** card swap (historical-artifact) of #3 ↑
- **Original:** A candlelit church zoetrope whose soldier's walk cycle has one frame per name on the parish memorial, cranked unbroken from the war diary's zero hour to its closing hour while each name read and answered is lifted from the drum, because the congregation watches its own responses break a man's stride until only a motionless still remains.

**prosecutor** (minor, rationale): The idea's core device, a memorial where names are taken out one by one so a sequence visibly degrades, has memorial-art cousins, though none with a zoetrope or call and response.

> The catalog's Stairs 1 Geneva links real-time-only and liturgy. It is a Greenaway work built from a hundred ephemeral live performances that were framed through peepholes, with a viewer physically present. That is a distant cousin: it shares the live, unrecorded, viewer-present frame but has no zoetrope, war diary or response ritual. The web searches found no zoetrope or phenomenakistoscope memorial in which frames are removed per name. They found only adjacent memorial practice. The Bundeswehr Cenotaph in Berlin shows names one at a time and is described as closer to a video installation than a conventional memorial. The Thiepval Memorial has names filled in with cement, which leaves gaps in the lists. Both are gap-or-erasure memorial conventions, so the idea's central device, felt gaps as the dead are named, has precedent in spirit. The literal combination (hand-cranked zoetrope, Office of the Dead, WO 95 diary hours, frame count equal to the names) was not found. The idea is therefore mostly novel, and only its general strategy is anticipated.

*Search: 19 results*

- [[stairs-1-geneva]] (Stairs 1 Geneva): Catalog work: a live, viewer-present, unrecorded cinema-as-event installation. It is a distant cousin that shares the live and ritual framing but has no zoetrope, no names and no war diary.
- [Bundeswehr Cenotaph](https://www.visitberlin.de/en/bundeswehr-cenotaph): A memorial installation that treats the names of the fallen one at a time, each for a few seconds, and is framed as video-installation-like, so it is close to the idea's naming-as-sequence concept.
- [Thiepval Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiepval_Memorial): A memorial whose lists of names have gaps from the removal of individual names, a distant parallel to the gaps in the drum.

*Advocate rebuts:* Agreed that showing names one at a time and leaving gaps in a memorial are established conventions, and that the combination of literal zoetrope, Office of the Dead, WO 95 hours and one frame per name has not been found. That is why the severity is minor. The revision also leaves the shared ground. It no longer erases or leaves gaps; it accumulates, and the dead are assembled by the response. So the strategy that has precedent, felt absence as the names are read, is gone, and the remaining cousins share only the general frame of live, unrecorded viewing that the real-time card demands.

**skeptic** (serious, behavior): The piece cannot be staged by one person, since a two-metre hand-cranked drum needs a parish, a nave, sidesmen and a congregation for hours of live cranking.

> The idea needs a real parish, a consecrated nave, an officiant, sidesmen to crank a two-metre drum for hours, and a congregation that keeps answering. A solo builder with evenings and weekends has none of these, and the drum itself is beyond the 3D printer and the basic soldering listed. The build also has to survive a live run of several hours of cranking, which nobody can rehearse alone.

*Advocate rebuts:* The critique judges a liturgical piece as if it were solo product engineering. The liturgy card is literally call and response, and a response needs a congregation in the same way a hymn needs singers. A congregation and a nave are not things you build. They are the venue, and asking a vicar for an Office of the Dead is a letter, not a budget line. What one person has to make is a plywood ring on a bicycle-hub bearing, a crank, card sleeves and a few hundred hand-drawn frames. That is shed work, not 3D printing or soldering. Frame spacing and speed can be tested on a small mock drum at home. Hours of cranking is a rota, not a feat of engineering. That the full run cannot be rehearsed is the real-time card working as it should.

**taste_critic** (serious, rationale): The mechanism is a one-to-one allegory announced by its own first stutters, so the hours-long vigil only confirms a solemn, expected point about remembrance and loss without any real strangeness.

> Meeting this thing, you stand in a candlelit nave and watch a soldier's walk stutter, then freeze. You understand within about ten minutes, long before the hours run out, that each response removes a frame and the man is being unmade by remembrance. After that the long vigil is just waiting for the stated outcome. This is the war-memorial elegy in its most approved form: names, candles, a lone soldier, stillness as death, the Last Post hush. It is solemn and well made, and it is the register every Remembrance service already trusts. Nothing in it flirts with the uncanny or the absurd. The one genuinely strange note, that the last frame is the name never read, gets buried under a tidy moral. The reading 'your prayers dismantle him' is a clean irony with a legible thesis, and the thesis does the work that the image should. A real zoetrope wobbling in a church could be eerie and wrong. Here every hitch is justified, so the dread has nowhere to go. The congregation is cast as quietly complicit, which flatters them with guilt instead of unsettling them. You leave moved in the expected way and forget it by the car park. The symbolic system is airtight and so inert: one name equals one frame, one response equals one removal, one stillness equals one death.

*Advocate concedes:* This is right, and it is the real problem. One response removing one frame, ending in one stillness meaning one death, is airtight. The congregation decodes it in minutes and then waits hours for an approved elegy, and the guilt it hands them is the comfortable kind. The strange thing was in the medium all along, and I pointed it the wrong way. If each answered name adds a frame, then a prayer for eternal rest is what makes a dead man get up and walk. That is a revenant, not a lament. The early drum also stops being legible: scattered frames give flaring, ghosting fragments, not a man limping. The ending becomes contingent, because the drum coasts to rest wherever it stops and leaves him caught mid-stride. The point is no longer stated by the mechanism. It is a contradiction the congregation is left to carry.

### 2. #13: A live Kriegsspiel requiem that re-fights a parish's memorial battle at 1:1 time with each block secretly bearing a dead man's name, read when the umpire's dice strike it, because commanders shut in the vestries learn their losses only by hearing the congregation answer 'Grant them eternal rest' through the wall.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1621 · feasibility 1445 · promise 1639
- **Cards:** real-time only, Kriegsspiel, liturgy
- **Lineage:** carried #3
- **Original:** A live-projected Kriegsspiel requiem that re-fights a parish's memorial battle at 1:1 time on an 1824 map table, the umpire's casualty dice calling each dead man's name for the congregation to answer 'Grant them eternal rest,' because chance deciding who is named restores the arbitrariness and complicity a tidy alphabetical war memorial erases.

**prosecutor** (minor, subject): No prior art was located for the specific combination; the nearest unverified cousins are separate Kriegsspiel reconstructions and name-reading memorials, and none was confirmed.

> The catalog holds no work linking any two of the three cards. My web searches failed. The search tool hit its usage limit, so I got no results and have no URL I can cite. I won't invent precedent. Kriegsspiel has been staged as a game, and names of the dead are read in memorial services. Those are separate neighbours, and I can't point to any one work that combines them.

- No prior art found.

*Advocate rebuts:* No located precedent is not a failure of the idea. Staging Kriegsspiel and reading names of the dead are separate practices, and the prosecutor confirms nothing joins them. The revision keeps that junction, made tighter: in the revision, the names are bound to the blocks themselves.

**skeptic** (serious, behavior): The 1:1 clock needs an hour-by-hour record of a small parish engagement, which a lone builder cannot source or validate in three months.

> The idea depends on an hour-by-hour timeline of one parish's memorial engagement so the game can run at 1:1 with the real fighting. Most small local actions have no such record. Finding the sources, reading them in period German or regional archives, and reconciling them with terrain and unit positions is research that takes far longer than the builder's roughly three months of evenings and weekends. Without that timeline, the clock that ends the service is invented, and the core claim that chance restores the real dead's arrangement falls apart.

*Advocate rebuts:* This misreads the clock. The game is re-fought freely, not replayed move by move against a historical timeline, so only two facts are needed: when the engagement began and when it ended. The idea never claimed chance restores the real order of deaths. It claims the opposite: chance replaces the order with arbitrariness. Parish memorials are overwhelmingly First World War, and battalion war diaries recording zero hour and the end of the action are digitised and in English (UK National Archives WO 95). Matching a memorial name to its battalion and that day is an evenings-and-weekends job. The one fair point is that I must choose an engagement whose diary fixes both hours, and the revision says so.

**taste_critic** (serious, rationale): The dice and names are staged as a controlled, pre-explained ritual, so the promised arbitrariness and complicity are announced as a thesis instead of ever ambushing anyone, leaving a tasteful, earnest memorial-art lecture.

> On paper this is dreamlike and heartbreaking: dice, miniature blocks, and great-grandfathers' names. But the thing itself will be a quiet church service where a man shoves wooden blocks around a table and a priest reads names off a list, and the congregation mumbles a response. The surreal collision never happens in the room, because the sacred and the procedural are kept politely apart. The dice choose names from a roll, and the roll was already printed on the memorial. So the 'arbitrariness' is a ceremony of arbitrariness, announced in the program and carried out in a plainly orderly way. Nobody is surprised, and nobody is implicated. The commanders playing 'their own great-grandfathers' are told in advance it is a play, so complicity is a stated theme rather than a felt one. Meanwhile the rationale does most of the work. The idea explains in paragraphs why it should hurt, which is how a thesis-driven installation behaves. It is the familiar 'war memorial reinterpreted by process' move, the participatory-commemoration cliché, with a gimmick bolted on: real-time clock, projector cut-off, silent reading. The cut-off at the battle's hour is the one good beat, but it is a single stage effect inside a long ritual. Nothing in the piece is odd or uncanny. A pair of vestry runners and an umpire read as historical-reenactment society chic. You meet it, you nod, you admire the conceit, and you go home having been shown an argument rather than haunted.

*Advocate concedes:* Right, and the fault runs deeper than the critique says. The names come from a list the officiant reads in turn, so the dice only ever chose when a name is read, never whose name. Commanders who are told the theme are play-acting complicity. The cure is inside Kriegsspiel itself. Commanders were kept apart and learned their losses only through the umpire. So I hide one name under each block, give the commanders no briefing, and let the congregation's 'Grant them eternal rest' through the vestry wall be their only casualty report. Then the commander is ambushed mid-order by a prayer for a man their own order killed, and the room's silences and volleys of names follow the fighting rather than a script.

### 3. #4: A live, unrecorded single-take hive film projected in each Kriegsspiel commander's shed, where audiences watch him read the comb as his only casualty report while the umpire tells the bees each struck block's memorial name, because a colony's alarm mixes his losses with its own weather and moods, so commanders press attacks on a roar they cannot decode.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1571 · feasibility 1318 · promise 1620
- **Cards:** real-time only, Kriegsspiel, beekeeping
- **Lineage:** card swap (domain) of #3 ↓
- **Original:** A live, unrecorded single-take film of the inside of an observation hive that runs while a Kriegsspiel re-fights a parish's war-memorial battle at 1:1 time, its umpire telling the bees each struck block's hidden name, because a colony's alarm builds and decays on its own clock, so the commanders' only casualty report is a roar nobody can script or stop.

**prosecutor** (serious, rationale): The hive-as-unscriptable-live-signal and telling-the-bees-for-war-dead pieces are both well-trodden; only the umpire-to-hive casualty channel inside a Kriegsspiel is untested, so the idea's novelty rests on a single coupling.

> I found no work that combines a live Kriegsspiel with a hive. The hive half is well covered, though. Ludwig Berger's 'Bien' (2019) puts contact mics in the comb and sends the signal in real time into a shed. Wolfgang Buttress's 'The Hive' lets live colony activity drive sound and light. The Sound Beehive Experiment adds contact mics and cameras inside a hive. 'Telling the Bees' is already a recurring art device. The Requiem for a Village page has the parish hive draped in black, a knock three times, and names spoken to the bees. It also says the hive grows louder with each message. Other works use an observation hive as a memorial or mourning site: Evergreen-Washelli's cemetery hive, Adelpha's 'Telling the Bees', and Louise Ann Wilson's 'Tell it to the Bees', which is a WWI-themed trail. The hive-as-live-signal and telling-the-bees-for-war-dead pieces are therefore both prior art. What remains new is the Kriegsspiel umpire loop, which routes casualty reports through the hive. The strict real-time rule and the 'nothing kept' ending add little on their own, because live-only hive installations already exist.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded. Contact-miked hives in sheds, live colony-driven installations, and telling the bees for the dead, including WWI dead, are all prior art. Claiming the unscriptable roar was the novelty was wrong. The prior art does leave one thing untouched: none of those works makes the hive a decision channel. In them the hive is listened to. Here it is acted on, because a commander must order men forward or back based on it, and the next deaths depend on how he reads it. That is not a thin coupling. It is the whole idea, so the revision makes it the centre and drops the borrowed parts as selling points.

**skeptic** (serious, card_reading `kriegsspiel`): The idea substitutes a 1824-style map table for the literal 1824 Prussian wargame, so it does not use the real game its card names.

> The idea swaps the literal 1824 Kriegsspiel for a 1824-style table, but the card requires the real game's apparatus and rules. The builder would have to source or faithfully rebuild Reisswitz's kit, including its scale and dice conventions, before the umpire's casualty dice mean anything. The idea never says how that will be done.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded. '1824-style' was a dodge. The fix is concrete: Reisswitz's 1824 Anleitung exists in translation, and its blocks, map scale and casualty dice can be reproduced exactly. The revision plays those rules as written, on a map of the real ground drawn at the rule's scale. It also gains something. 1916 deaths are decided by 1824 dice, and the anachronism is visible to anyone who knows the game.

**taste_critic** (serious, behavior): The audience sees only anonymous bees in infrared while the war, names and knocks stay hidden, leaving a solemn heritage-memorial vigil whose 'bees as grief' metaphor is the first thing anyone would reach for.

> On paper this is catnip: bees, names of the dead, crepe, sheds, a roar nobody can script. But what the audience actually meets is a dark room and a long infrared shot of bees on a comb, with the whole war hidden from them. The one image is a nocturnal-documentary texture with occasional shudders, and the knocks and names happen offstage, so the audience can't connect any shudder to a death. The symbolic engine (name, block, knock, roar) works only for the two commanders in sheds, so the film is a spectator's view of someone else's piece. Its feeling is solemn, reverent and tasteful: the 'war memorial plus nature plus ritual' register that heritage-art commemorations already use. Telling the bees is a well-worn folk-horror and elegy trope, and a hive swelling with grief is the obvious metaphor for loss. Nothing in it is uncanny or absurd. The absurd chance is that a colony is calm or roars for reasons of weather, queen state or smoke, but the idea insists the roar is a faithful casualty report, so it sells a legible meaning it can't guarantee. A real idea is buried here, in two men in sheds going mad over a sound they cannot interpret. The finished thing stages instead a sanctified, hushed vigil, and 'nothing is kept' reads as an authenticity badge more than a payoff. Forgettable in the way respectful memorial art is: affecting for an hour, then it dissolves into 'bees as grief'.

*Advocate concedes:* Conceded, and this critique names the better idea. The audience was shut out of the only engine that worked. I was also selling the roar as a faithful casualty count when a colony roars for its own reasons. Both faults become the piece: the audience now sits in the sheds behind the commanders, and the roar's unreliability is the point. A man has to choose whether to attack based on a signal that is partly his dead and partly the weather. That is uncanny and cruel rather than reverent. Watching him guess is the film.
