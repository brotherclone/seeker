- **Original:** A live-projected Kriegsspiel requiem that re-fights a parish's memorial battle at 1:1 time on an 1824 map table, the umpire's casualty dice calling each dead man's name for the congregation to answer 'Grant them eternal rest,' because chance deciding who is named restores the arbitrariness and complicity a tidy alphabetical war memorial erases.

> The catalog holds no work linking any two of the three cards. My web searches failed. The search tool hit its usage limit, so I got no results and have no URL I can cite. I won't invent precedent. Kriegsspiel has been staged as a game, and names of the dead are read in memorial services. Those are separate neighbours, and I can't point to any one work that combines them.

- No prior art found.

*Advocate rebuts:* No located precedent is not a failure of the idea. Staging Kriegsspiel and reading names of the dead are separate practices, and the prosecutor confirms nothing joins them. The revision keeps that junction, made tighter: in the revision, the names are bound to the blocks themselves.

**skeptic** (serious, behavior): The 1:1 clock needs an hour-by-hour record of a small parish engagement, which a lone builder cannot source or validate in three months.

> The idea depends on an hour-by-hour timeline of one parish's memorial engagement so the game can run at 1:1 with the real fighting. Most small local actions have no such record. Finding the sources, reading them in period German or regional archives, and reconciling them with terrain and unit positions is research that takes far longer than the builder's roughly three months of evenings and weekends. Without that timeline, the clock that ends the service is invented, and the core claim that chance restores the real dead's arrangement falls apart.

*Advocate rebuts:* This misreads the clock. The game is re-fought freely, not replayed move by move against a historical timeline, so only two facts are needed: when the engagement began and when it ended. The idea never claimed chance restores the real order of deaths. It claims the opposite: chance replaces the order with arbitrariness. Parish memorials are overwhelmingly First World War, and battalion war diaries recording zero hour and the end of the action are digitised and in English (UK National Archives WO 95). Matching a memorial name to its battalion and that day is an evenings-and-weekends job. The one fair point is that I must choose an engagement whose diary fixes both hours, and the revision says so.

**taste_critic** (serious, rationale): The dice and names are staged as a controlled, pre-explained ritual, so the promised arbitrariness and complicity are announced as a thesis instead of ever ambushing anyone, leaving a tasteful, earnest memorial-art lecture.

> On paper this is dreamlike and heartbreaking: dice, miniature blocks, and great-grandfathers' names. But the thing itself will be a quiet church service where a man shoves wooden blocks around a table and a priest reads names off a list, and the congregation mumbles a response. The surreal collision never happens in the room, because the sacred and the procedural are kept politely apart. The dice choose names from a roll, and the roll was already printed on the memorial. So the 'arbitrariness' is a ceremony of arbitrariness, announced in the program and carried out in a plainly orderly way. Nobody is surprised, and nobody is implicated. The commanders playing 'their own great-grandfathers' are told in advance it is a play, so complicity is a stated theme rather than a felt one. Meanwhile the rationale does most of the work. The idea explains in paragraphs why it should hurt, which is how a thesis-driven installation behaves. It is the familiar 'war memorial reinterpreted by process' move, the participatory-commemoration cliché, with a gimmick bolted on: real-time clock, projector cut-off, silent reading. The cut-off at the battle's hour is the one good beat, but it is a single stage effect inside a long ritual. Nothing in the piece is odd or uncanny. A pair of vestry runners and an umpire read as historical-reenactment society chic. You meet it, you nod, you admire the conceit, and you go home having been shown an argument rather than haunted.

*Advocate concedes:* Right, and the fault runs deeper than the critique says. The names come from a list the officiant reads in turn, so the dice only ever chose when a name is read, never whose name. Commanders who are told the theme are play-acting complicity. The cure is inside Kriegsspiel itself. Commanders were kept apart and learned their losses only through the umpire. So I hide one name under each block, give the commanders no briefing, and let the congregation's 'Grant them eternal rest' through the vestry wall be their only casualty report. Then the commander is ambushed mid-order by a prayer for a man their own order killed, and the room's silences and volleys of names follow the fighting rather than a script.

### 2. #2: A hand-pushed hooded camera obscura whose hidden reader recites a parish's old written bounds against the live inverted image at Rogationtide, the walkers answering the litany where the named landmark still shows and 'Amen' to the landmark-curse wherever something else stands, their own houses included, because the unrecordable image turns the gap between record and land into a live verdict.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1661 · feasibility 1581 · promise 1643
- **Cards:** real-time only, camera obscura, liturgy
- **Lineage:** reworking of #10 ↑
- **Original:** A handcart camera obscura carried around a parish boundary at Rogationtide, its hooded reader calling out each landmark sliding across the live table so walkers answer with the litany, or with the curse when a fence blocks the old bounds, because beating the bounds was memory without maps and an unrecordable moving image puts that memory back at stake.

**prosecutor** (minor, constraint): The live-only, unrecordable image is not a real constraint, since every camera obscura already works this way, so the idea's rationale depends on the liturgy pairing and adds nothing of its own.

> I could not run web searches (the tool hit its use limit), so I have no verified URLs to cite. The catalog also holds no work linking any two of the cards. From general knowledge, the parts exist separately. Walk-in and portable camera obscuras on carts, trailers and vans are a familiar format in art and touring exhibitions. Beating the bounds has been revived in many parishes and in contemporary walking-art projects. The Commination service already includes the curse on those who remove landmarks, with the 'Amen' response. The literal combination of a handcart camera obscura used as a boundary-beating liturgy appears new to me, but I did not confirm that. The live-only constraint is also inherent to any camera obscura, so it adds little.

- No prior art found.

*Advocate rebuts:* It is true that every camera obscura is live-only. That is why it is the right instrument, not a weakness. In the home field of film, the default move is to shoot the procession and keep it. The constraint rules out the documentary, the archive and the replay, and that matches what beating the bounds was: a record that existed only while it was being walked. The prosecutor found no prior art for the combination, so the constraint does not need to be novel. It only needs to bind, and it does. The revision binds it harder: the old written bounds are read against an image that cannot be kept, so the comparison happens once, in public, and leaves nothing behind.

**skeptic** (serious, card_reading `camera-obscura`): The walk-in box with a rotating lens-and-mirror turret cannot keep the passing boundary in focus across its range, so the live image the rite depends on goes soft.

> A walk-in camera obscura is a room-sized light-tight box, and the image only works if the optics are right. A rotating lens-and-mirror turret on a handcart has to sweep across uneven parish ground while the projected landmarks stay in focus. Objects at 2 m and 40 m need different focus, and a fixed lens of usable size will blur one range or the other. Getting a large-aperture lens and a precision mirror in a turret that rotates smoothly means buying optics and a bearing assembly the builder does not have, then building a cart that carries a box and a person over the bounds. That is a months-long optical project, not a weekend build.

*Advocate concedes:* The rotating turret and the walk-in room were over-built, and I concede that part. Still, the focus problem is not fatal. The cart travels along the boundary, so the boundary is always on one side. A fixed side-facing mirror does the job, and there is nothing to bear or rotate. A single long-focal-length lens, a cheap large magnifier or a spectacle-blank meniscus, gives usable depth across hedge distance. The reader racks focus by sliding the lens in a cardboard or tin tube, which is how eighteenth-century tent and box obscuras were worked. Landmarks at 2 m and 40 m are not seen at the same moment, so the reader focuses on each as it is called. A hooded box the reader stands under replaces the walk-in room. That is plywood, a mirror and a lens on a garden cart, so a weekend build, not a months-long optical project.

**taste_critic** (serious, behavior): It stages a tidy heritage re-enactment in which the weird inverted image only illustrates a moral about fences, so the uncanny, dreamlike potential of the cart and the chant is spent on civic good-feeling.

> Meeting the finished thing: a few heritage-minded people push a cart through a village at dusk while a man in a box reads out 'oak, stone, culvert' and the group mumbles a response. That is a re-enactment, the pageant tradition with a clever optical prop. Camera obscura as 'quaint Victorian wonder' and beating the bounds as 'charming folk custom' are both already in the cozy-heritage drawer, and the idea puts them side by side without letting either one break open. The uncanny is available and unused: an inverted, ghostly, upside-down parish sliding across a table while voices chant is potentially eerie. But the idea reads it as civic instruction. The curse is the one sharp element, yet it is pointed at a polite target, a neighbour's fence, and answered with a dutiful 'Amen'. It becomes a petition about planning disputes in liturgical dress, a local-politics gesture with a moral already attached. The reader names what the image shows, and the image only confirms what is already known, a boundary and a landmark. Nothing slips, misreads, or haunts. The rationale also argues that the piece matters because 'the old rite's purpose is real again', which is a justification pitch. It sounds like the project statement for a heritage-arts grant, and the piece itself has no dream logic or absurdity. It would be remembered as a nice afternoon, not as something that got under the skin.

*Advocate concedes:* This critique is right. The image only confirmed what everyone already knew, and the curse was aimed at a safe neighbour, so the piece ended as a planning petition in liturgical dress. The sharper idea was already inside the setup. The reader in the box cannot see the walkers, and the walkers cannot see the image, so the rite rests on an unseen, inverted picture that only one voice reports. In the revision he reads the parish's old written bounds against that live image. Where they disagree, the walkers bless an oak that isn't there and say 'Amen' to a curse that falls on whatever stands in its place, including their own houses and the church hall. The image now contradicts the text instead of illustrating it. The heritage comfort breaks, because the people taking part are the ones being judged.

### 3. #4: A camera obscura tent that blazons each visitor as they cross the projected lists outside, so on entering they hear their own arms repeated once by the strangers who watched them, then sit to blazon the next, until dusk leaves only 'sable, plain', because heraldry identifies faceless people by colour and device, which is all the obscura shows.

- **Ratings:** novelty 1641 · feasibility 1354 · promise 1591
- **Cards:** real-time only, camera obscura, heraldry
- **Lineage:** card swap (domain) of #10 ↑
- **Original:** A camera obscura tent where a herald blazons each stranger crossing the live projected street as a coat of arms, granted aloud and never written, until dusk erases tinctures and the last arms are 'sable, plain', because heraldry was invented to identify faceless strangers by colour and device, which is exactly all the obscura shows.

**prosecutor** (minor, behavior): No exact precedent was found, though the idea's reliance on tincture loss at dusk might echo existing obscura and heraldic art practice that I could not verify.

> I could not run any searches, so I have no verified prior art to cite. The catalog also holds no work linking two of the cards. I know of camera obscura performances and of heraldry-as-art projects, but I can't point to a page showing the literal combination of a spoken, unrecorded blazon of strangers seen through an obscura. Any precedent I claimed would be invented.

- No prior art found.

*Advocate rebuts:* No precedent was found, so this charge has nothing in it to answer. The tincture loss at dusk is not borrowed from an art practice. It is the Purkinje shift: in failing light reds go dark first and blues hold longest, while light and dark contrast outlasts hue. Any obscura table shows this at dusk. The structure stays as it is, and the revision keeps that ending.

**skeptic** (serious, card_reading `camera-obscura`): A portable tent cannot hold a sharp street image across a rotating turret without a long throw that a carried, single-person tent cannot physically accommodate.

> A pinhole or meniscus obscura projects a sharp image only near its focal plane. To hold focus across a street at varying distances, the tent needs a long focal length, which means a large box or a long throw. A rotating mirror turret adds alignment and vibration problems. The builder can buy the optics, but a lens and mirror large enough to cover a street will not fit a portable tent that one person can carry and set up each evening.

*Advocate concedes:* The rotating turret should go. Panning across a street at varying distances creates exactly the focus, alignment and vibration problems the skeptic describes. The other claims go too far. Tent obscuras go back to Kepler, and for a fixed subject distance a lens of about one metre focal length focused at 15 to 20 metres has workable depth of field. The image only has to show colour and silhouette, never faces. So the revision fixes the lens on one marked stretch of pavement at a known distance and drops the one-person-carry promise. It becomes a tent pitched by two people, not a backpack.

**taste_critic** (serious, behavior): Every blazon is a mechanical colour-to-tincture translation of anonymous passersby who never know, so the ritual produces cute pedantry and one tidy dusk punchline, not any felt tension between honour and surveillance.

> Meeting it: you sit in a dark tent, a person in a tabard murmurs 'Per pale azure and gules, a bicycle Or' about a blur on a table, and the bench audience repeats it. It is charming for two minutes. After that you have the joke, and the joke is a lookup table: coat colour becomes tincture, bag becomes charge, direction becomes dexter or sinister. The herald is a translator, not a character. Nobody in the street knows, so the 'ennobling' has no recipient, no reaction, no cost. The rationale claims surveillance and honour at once, but nothing stages that tension. It is asserted in the statement and never felt in the room. It lands in the well-worn genre of the quaint, erudite participatory art piece: costumed fussiness, a hushed ritual, a wink at pedantry. The dusk ending is the one good move, because tinctures failing is real loss, but it is a clock running down on a repetitive procedure. 'Sable, plain' is a neat last line that the whole piece is built to deliver, like a punchline, and the same format delivers it every evening. Nothing uncanny or heartbreaking happens, because the strangers stay abstract and the grants are interchangeable pleasantries.

*Advocate concedes:* This is the fatal one, and it is right. A grant with no recipient has no cost, the herald turns into a lookup table, and the tension between surveillance and honour is only asserted. The better idea is already inside it. Make the visitors the strangers. Each one walks the lists unknowing, then enters and hears their own arms once from people who watched them faceless and upside down. They cannot verify the blazon, and then they become the court for the next person. Now everyone is both watched and watcher. The grants are no longer interchangeable, because each lands on the person in front of you. The dusk clock becomes an unfairness felt in the queue, so 'sable, plain' is no longer a punchline. It is a sentence passed on whoever came last.
